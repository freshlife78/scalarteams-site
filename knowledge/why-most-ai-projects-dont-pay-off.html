Why most AI projects don’t pay off — and what the few that do get right

Published October 31, 2026 by Scalar Teams

Over the past two years almost every company has tried something with AI. Very few can point to a line in their financial statements and show where it moved. That gap between activity and results is now the main finding of the research, and it is worth understanding before you fund the next pilot.

The pattern in the research

Boston Consulting Group publishes an ongoing series called “How AI-First Companies are Winning,” written one industry at a time. Read together, the summaries tell a consistent story. The private equity piece, dated January 23, 2026, opens by stating that most private equity firms cannot show meaningful returns from AI in many of their portfolio companies. The biopharma piece from January 2, 2026 says most companies are running pilots and only some are starting to scale them. The telecom piece from February 2026 frames the task as moving beyond today’s cost-cutting pilots.

The academic picture is similar. Fortune’s report on MIT NANDA’s 2025 study, “The GenAI Divide,” says roughly 5 percent of pilots achieve rapid revenue acceleration while the vast majority stall with little or no measurable effect on profit and loss. We look at that study in detail in a separate article, including its small sample.

Vendors see the same thing from the inside. OpenAI’s business guide on finding and scaling use cases cites McKinsey figures showing that 92 percent of companies plan to increase their AI investment while only 1 percent believe their AI investments have reached full maturity. A secondary source, the engineering firm &above, summarizes Kyndryl’s 2026 People Readiness Report as finding that 57 percent of enterprises have AI embedded in core processes or deployed broadly, up from 35 percent a year earlier, yet only 11 percent have achieved both of their top two AI objectives.

Why the pilots stall

None of these sources blames the technology. The explanations are organizational, and three of them repeat.

The tool was the plan. A team buys or builds something, switches it on, and waits. BCG’s piece on retail banking puts the condition plainly: AI can transform how banks operate “but only if they fundamentally rewire how work is done.” A chatbot bolted onto an unchanged process is still the unchanged process.

Nobody owned the result. Pilots are often run by an innovation group or a vendor, while the manager who lives with the process has no stake in the outcome. When the pilot ends, so does the attention.

The pilot never touched work that is measured. Fortune reports that MIT found more than half of generative AI budgets going to sales and marketing tools, while the clearest returns came from back-office automation such as replacing outsourced processing and cutting external agency costs. If a pilot sits where nothing is counted, nothing can be shown.

What the companies that get value do differently

Reading across the same sources, the successful minority share a few habits.

  • They redesign the workflow around the tool. BCG’s airline piece, dated September 29, 2025, argues that airlines willing to redesign workflows with AI at the core will set the pace for the industry.
  • They start where the work is already measured. The back-office finding above is the practical version of this. Pick a process with a queue, a cycle time or an error count, and you will know within weeks whether anything changed.
  • Leadership owns it. OpenAI’s guide lists leadership direction as its first principle for finding use cases and names top-down support as a hallmark of deployments that succeed. It means someone senior is accountable for a business number, not for a demo.
  • They are realistic about building versus buying. Fortune reports that in MIT’s dataset, purchasing from specialized vendors or partnering succeeded about 67 percent of the time, while internal builds succeeded about one third as often. The lesson for a mid-sized company is not “never build.” It is to build only the thin layer that is specific to your operation and buy the rest.
  • They scale deliberately. BCG’s biopharma and telecom pieces both frame the task as getting beyond pilots. A pilot that works in one branch has to be made boring enough to run in all of them.

Limits of this evidence

Two cautions. BCG is a consultancy that sells AI transformation work, and its series is written for large enterprises, so its examples will not map directly onto a 40-person distributor. The Kyndryl figures reach us through a secondary source that also sells embedded engineering. And we were unable to load McKinsey’s State of AI page while preparing this article, so none of the figures above are taken from it directly; the McKinsey numbers quoted here are the ones OpenAI reproduces in its own guide.

How we apply this

Our method borrows from the Toyota Production System, so we read research like this as a checklist for practice rather than a forecast. In order, the steps are: go and see, map, measure waste, prioritize, build small, standardize and review.

The research says payoff comes from changing measured work with an accountable owner. In practice that means we begin with go and see: standing with the people who do the job, not with a software demo. We map the process as it actually runs, including the spreadsheet somebody keeps on the side. We measure waste in the Toyota sense, counting waiting, rework, handoffs and re-keying, so there is a baseline before anything is automated. Only then do we prioritize, and we prefer the back-office candidates the evidence favours: the ones with a queue and a cycle time.

We build small, which means one process, one owner, one number to move. When it works we standardize it, writing the procedure down so the result does not depend on the engineer who built it, and we review it with the process owner on a fixed cadence. None of this guarantees a return. It does make the return, or its absence, visible.
