Where to look for AI opportunities in your operation

Published October 31, 2026 by Scalar Teams

Most owners we speak with do not lack ideas for AI. They lack a way to tell a good idea from a distraction. OpenAI published a guide for businesses, “Identifying and scaling AI use cases,” that offers a plain method for this. It is a vendor document, but the method underneath is sound and maps closely to how an operations person already thinks.

Where the guide comes from

OpenAI says the guide draws on 300 of its most successful customer implementations, more than 4,000 adoption surveys and over 2 million business users. The six categories of use case it describes come from an analysis of more than 600 use cases sourced from customers. The examples are all OpenAI customers and most are large companies, which is worth remembering as you read. The method, though, needs no particular vendor.

Three places to look

The guide suggests looking for AI opportunities in three kinds of workplace friction.

Repetitive, low-value tasks. Every job carries manual work that pulls people away from the parts that matter: summarizing meetings, hunting for trends in a spreadsheet, answering the same question for the tenth time. The guide quotes a product executive who keeps a list of tasks she no longer does herself and asks, whenever a chore annoys her, “how can I not have to do this again?”

Skill bottlenecks. Work slows when someone hits the edge of their expertise and has to wait for another department. The guide gives data analysis, design, on-brand writing and web development as examples. For a distributor, the equivalent is the operations manager who needs a quick report but has to queue for the one person who knows the system.

Navigating ambiguity. Open-ended work stalls because people do not know how to start. The guide describes teams using AI to brainstorm, find quick patterns in raw data, or propose next steps when the path is unclear.

The guide’s practical suggestion is to ask each team to list the tasks that fit these three descriptions. It cites the chief financial officer of a sports betting company who asked every member of the finance team to describe processes they thought could benefit, then turned the list into a roadmap.

Six basic things the tools do

To help staff recognize opportunities, the guide groups use cases into six basic types it calls primitives: content creation, research, coding, data analysis, ideation and strategy, and automation. Each comes with department-level examples. For finance the guide lists drafting policy documents and accounting memos for expert review, searching for benchmarks and accounting guidance, writing Python scripts to automate parts of the monthly close, analyzing expense data across spreadsheets, and turning weekly financial data into an executive summary with alerts.

Two of the guide’s customer stories are relevant to operators. A life sciences company reports saving 135 hours in its first six months by using the tool for first-draft email campaigns. A fashion marketplace used the tool to generate the code that reconciles millions of spreadsheet rows for its performance analysis, then used it to draft weekly performance reports. Both are modest, measurable, back-office wins.

Deciding what to do first

Once teams start listing ideas, the guide says, the problem flips from finding use cases to choosing among them. Its answer is a two-by-two grid scoring each idea on value to the company and effort to build.

  • High value, low effort. Quick wins and the best place to start building momentum.
  • Low value, low effort. Self-service tasks a single person can set up for themselves; some grow into team tools.
  • High value, high effort. Often transformational, but requiring more time, planning and resources. The guide suggests using the quick wins to earn the right to attempt these.
  • Low value, high effort. Set aside, and revisit as the tools improve.

Two further pieces of advice: re-score the list every quarter, because today’s high-effort idea may become low-effort as capabilities improve. And have leaders champion the use cases that affect whole departments, since the guide names top-down support as a key hallmark of deployments that succeed.

From tasks to workflows

The guide’s final section observes that teams start with individual tasks and that power users eventually find use cases spanning several steps. Its suggestion is to have those users break a workflow into its tasks, identify which primitive each one is, and map the sequence. That is process mapping under another name, and it is the step that turns scattered time savings into a changed process.

Keep in mind

This is a sales document from a vendor whose product appears in every example, the examples are self-reported and selected, and the guide itself says that building an AI-ready culture and driving adoption are out of its scope. The document carries no publication date. Use it for the method, not as evidence of what you will achieve.

How we apply this

Our method borrows from the Toyota Production System, so we read research like this as a checklist for practice rather than a forecast. In order, the steps are: go and see, map, measure waste, prioritize, build small, standardize and review.

The guide’s three places to look are a reasonable starting list, but we do not take them from a survey. We go and see: we spend time on the floor and in the back office watching the work, because the repetitive task people have stopped noticing is rarely the one they write down. We map the process so each task sits in its sequence, and we measure the waste in minutes, touches and errors rather than in enthusiasm.

Our prioritization grid is the guide’s with one addition: we weight ideas by whether the result can be measured, since an unmeasurable win cannot be standardized. We build the top item small, with the department’s own people owning it, and we review the ranking on a fixed cadence, which is the guide’s quarterly re-scoring turned into a habit.
