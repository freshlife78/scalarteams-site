The 95% problem: what MIT found about AI pilots

Published October 31, 2026 by Scalar Teams

In August 2025 a study from MIT’s NANDA initiative produced a number that has been repeated in boardrooms ever since: 95 percent of generative AI pilots at companies are failing. The headline is accurate to the report. It is also easy to misread. This article explains what the study measured, what its authors say explains the gap, and where the evidence is weaker than the headline suggests.

What the study measured

The report is titled “The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025.” Fortune’s Sheryl Estrada, who covered it on August 18, 2025, summarizes the central finding: about 5 percent of AI pilot programs achieve rapid revenue acceleration, while the vast majority stall with little or no measurable effect on profit and loss. The report describes that failure rate as “the clearest manifestation of the GenAI Divide.”

The key word is measurable. The study did not find that the tools were broken or that staff refused to use them. It found that most pilots could not show a change in the numbers a chief financial officer tracks. A pilot that made ten people a little faster, with no change in cost, cycle time or revenue, counts as a failure by this definition.

Legal.io, in a secondary write-up dated August 23, 2025, adds the adoption funnel the report describes. More than 80 percent of organizations had piloted general tools such as ChatGPT or Copilot and nearly 40 percent reported deploying them. Purpose-built enterprise systems fared worse: about 60 percent of firms evaluated one, 20 percent reached a pilot, and 5 percent reached production.

The learning gap

The report’s explanation for the divide is what it calls a learning gap. Fortune reports that the failures were attributed to flawed enterprise integration rather than to regulation or model performance. Aditya Challapally, the lead author, explained to Fortune that generic tools work well for individuals but stall inside companies because they do not learn from or adapt to the company’s workflows.

The tool that impresses you on a Saturday afternoon does not know your approval rules, your customer exceptions or the three systems your order data passes through. Unless something teaches it, it stays a clever stranger.

Where the money went versus where the returns were

Fortune reports that more than half of generative AI budgets in the study went to sales and marketing tools. The biggest returns, however, were in back-office automation: eliminating business process outsourcing, cutting external agency costs and streamlining operations. Legal.io’s summary makes the same point, noting that budgets favoured sales and marketing even though operations and finance showed better returns.

This is the finding most relevant to a small or mid-sized operator. The glamorous use cases attract the budget. The repetitive, well-defined work in accounts payable, order entry and document handling is where the study saw money actually saved.

Build or buy

According to Fortune, purchasing AI tools from specialized vendors or building partnerships succeeded about 67 percent of the time, while internal builds succeeded roughly one third as often. Challapally told Fortune that the research team found companies trying to build their own tools almost everywhere they went, and that purchased solutions delivered more reliable results. Legal.io summarizes the same finding as vendor tools succeeding about twice as often as internal builds, and adds that the successful firms chose vendors whose systems could be customized and could keep learning.

Shadow AI and the people side

Both write-ups highlight what the report calls shadow AI: employees using personal, unsanctioned tools for work. Legal.io reports that only 40 percent of companies had an official large language model subscription while 90 percent of surveyed workers reported daily use of personal AI tools for job tasks. Fortune adds that workforce changes were concentrated in customer support and administrative roles, and that companies were mostly not backfilling vacant positions rather than conducting layoffs. Fortune also notes that successful companies tended to empower line managers rather than rely only on a central AI lab.

Limitations worth knowing

This is a small study carrying a very large headline. Legal.io reports the sample as 52 executive interviews, surveys of 153 leaders and an analysis of 300 public AI deployments. Fortune’s article describes it as 150 interviews with leaders, a survey of 350 employees and 300 public deployments. We could not reconcile the two descriptions from the sources available to us, but on either count the survey base is in the low hundreds, and the 300 deployments are ones that were made public, which is its own selection effect.

Fortune also notes that surveyed companies were often hesitant to share failure rates, and Legal.io points out that the report does not explain how the sample was selected or exactly how the 95 percent was calculated. Legal.io’s own summary is inconsistent about whether the 5 percent refers to pilots or to integrated systems. Treat the number as a strong signal about direction, not as a precise measurement.

How we apply this

Our method borrows from the Toyota Production System, so we read research like this as a checklist for practice rather than a forecast. In order, the steps are: go and see, map, measure waste, prioritize, build small, standardize and review.

The study’s definition of failure is our definition of success turned around: a change you can see in the numbers. So before we build anything we go and see the process, map it end to end, and measure the waste in it, which gives us a baseline a CFO would accept. We prioritize the back-office work the study found most rewarding, the repetitive, rule-bound tasks in finance and operations.

We build small and we close the learning gap deliberately: the system is taught your approval rules and exceptions, and a named person on your team owns it, which matches the report’s point about line managers. Then we standardize the procedure and review it against the baseline. If the number has not moved, we say so, and that too is a result the study would recognize.
