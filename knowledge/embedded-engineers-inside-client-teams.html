Embedded engineers: why the AI industry is moving people inside client teams

Published October 31, 2026 by Scalar Teams

In 2026 the biggest names in AI reached the same conclusion from different directions: the technology was not the bottleneck anymore, the customer organization was. Their response was to put engineers inside those organizations. The job title is forward-deployed engineer. Understanding the model matters even if you never hire from a giant, because it shows what getting AI into real work takes.

What a forward-deployed engineer is

Forrester analyst Ashutosh Sharma, writing on July 30, 2026, describes forward-deployed engineers as embedded engineers who work inside a customer’s environment to build software and drive adoption, a model Palantir pioneered. &above, a secondary source whose author runs an embedded engineering firm, defines them more bluntly as senior engineers who sit inside the client’s team with the right to commit code, and notes the title has spread so widely in 2026 that the real questions are who holds those rights, what is left behind, and who owns the outcome afterward.

Brocoders, another secondary source from a company selling the model, frames the shift against traditional staff augmentation. Staff augmentation sells hours and capacity under the buyer’s management. A forward-deployed team owns the problem and carries the delivery risk. As its author puts it, “The unit of value is the outcome, not the timesheet.” Brocoders also cites the search firm Christian & Timbers for Indeed job postings with the title rising from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026.

Why the vendors are paying for it

Forrester’s account is the most concrete. Between May 4 and July 15, 2026, the post says, four technology providers committed roughly nine billion dollars to putting their own engineers inside customers’ organizations; the ventures it describes come from Anthropic, OpenAI, Amazon Web Services and Microsoft. Sharma’s explanation is that the constraint on growth is no longer model performance but organizational readiness: the vendors can sell the software, but the customer cannot absorb it.

He describes three shifts the model drives. Providers increasingly get paid on outcomes, taking on integration risk that used to sit with the customer. The work itself changes, because the engineer’s first job is to encode how the company actually makes decisions, knowledge that lives in spreadsheets, exception handling and the heads of experienced employees. And the embedded engineers feed customer constraints back into the vendor’s product roadmap.

Forrester’s clearest example is Uber: 30 engineers embedded in finance, legal and human resources, 16 small “agentic pods” running over two months, and financial pacing reports that dropped from two days to ten minutes. Forrester’s point is that Uber built that capability rather than renting it.

Where the model goes wrong

All three sources, including the two that sell the service, are candid about the risks.

Lock-in. Forrester warns that deep platform integration builds switching costs as data, workflows and institutional knowledge accumulate inside one vendor’s tools. &above makes the related point that engineers employed by a model provider are not neutral, since their job is to get you onto that provider’s platform.

Dependency. &above’s central argument is that a forward-deployed engineer will get your first AI workflow into production but will not, on their own, leave your organization able to build the next three without them. Its test for buyers: if the engagement ends in a renewal on the vendor’s product, “you have bought a deployment,” not a capability.

Reverting to projects. Forrester calls it the open question for the next 18 months whether service integrators can scale this work without reverting to project-shaped delivery.

Tacit knowledge is hard. Forrester notes that agents cannot read what is in people’s heads, so the encoding work is genuinely difficult.

What to ask before you hire one

Forrester’s advice to buyers is that temporary embedded engagements are the right answer today, since almost no enterprise can staff this itself, but that the goal should be a permanent, cross-functional capability you employ and hold accountable for outcomes. His measure: “count the provider’s engineers in your building, then count your own,” two years from now.

Brocoders offers an outcome test in three questions: who carries the risk, what you are billed for, and who owns the problem end to end. &above adds the organizational gaps to check for: a shared standard for evaluating results, an agreed definition of production-ready, and someone internal who can keep improving the system after the engineers leave.

Insist on a named business outcome, keep the orchestration and evaluation layers under your control, and make sure a person on your payroll is learning how the system works from day one.

A note on the sources

Two of the three sources are published by firms that sell embedded engineering, and Forrester’s post is analysis rather than survey research. The Uber and recruiter figures are reported second-hand. We include them because the direction they describe is consistent across sources with different interests, not because any single number is precise.

How we apply this

Our method borrows from the Toyota Production System, so we read research like this as a checklist for practice rather than a forecast. In order, the steps are: go and see, map, measure waste, prioritize, build small, standardize and review.

Our operations engineers work embedded for the reason Forrester gives: the knowledge that has to be encoded is tacit, and you only get it by going and seeing. So an engagement begins on your floor and in your systems, mapping the process as it runs and measuring the waste in it, before any tool is chosen. We prioritize with you, and we build small so that the first automation is one you can inspect and own.

We take the dependency warning seriously, so standardization is part of the work, not an afterthought: procedures written down, prompts and checks documented, and a named person on your team trained to run and change the system. We review with that person on a fixed cadence. The test we hold ourselves to is &above’s: at the end, you should have bought a capability, not a deployment.
